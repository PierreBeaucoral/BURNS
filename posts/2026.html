Every summer now brings the same headlines out of the Mediterranean, and the same question underneath them: is this year actually worse, or does it just feel that way? All summer, this page tracked the 2026 season week by week and refused to call it early. The season window closed on 30 September, so it is time for the verdict: how much burned, where, how 2026 ranks against the seasons before it, and what made it different.

Two numbers sum up the year, and they cover different time windows. Over the summer season, 1 June to 30 September, satellites mapped about 786,000 hectares of burned land in Europe, across 4,730 separate fire outlines. That is roughly 75 times the area of the city of Paris (about 105 km², or 10,500 hectares), and about 63% more than a typical recent summer (483,000 hectares). But 2026 did not start in June. Counting from 1 January, the total reaches about 967,000 hectares, because late winter and spring had already burned 181,000 hectares before most people start watching for fire season at all. That spring was not unusual, though: it ranks fifth of the last 10 years. The chart right below sticks to the summer window, so that seasons are compared like for like; the maps after it show everything mapped in 2026.

TipThe season in four lines
  • Rank: 2026 is the second-worst summer of the last 10 (2017 to 2026), behind only 2025.
  • Shape: an average spring, a violent July, a long August, then a September that stayed above normal.
  • Where: Spain carried the year, and France, Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina had their worst summer of the last ten.
  • How: a few giant fires made most of the total, as they do every year.

About the data, in one line: these are rapid satellite outlines from EFFIS, measured as burned area (never fire counts), and they will still move by a few percent before official figures come out. Hectares are the unit throughout; charts abbreviate thousands of hectares as kha.

NoteHow the data works (click to expand)

Satellites spot large fires, and Europe’s EFFIS service (part of the Copernicus programme) draws the outline of each burned area it can map. I work with those outlines. I measure each fire’s size directly from its shape, using a map projection that keeps areas honest, so hectares are comparable across countries. And I count only the parts that fall inside Europe. The method is the same as in the 2025 season analysis this project grew out of; that post remains the reference for the details.

  • Most mapped fires are small, not big. EFFIS once caught mainly fires of roughly 30 hectares and up, but sharper Sentinel-2 imagery now maps far smaller ones too, so most outlines on this page are in fact well under 30 hectares. The very smallest fires are still missed, so totals stay below official statistics.
  • The outlines are rapid estimates. They are revised as better satellite imagery arrives, even after the season ends. The totals below are final for this page, but they are not official statistics: expect EFFIS’s consolidated figures, published later, to differ by a few percent.
  • Dates are fire dates. Every date on this page is the day EFFIS records a fire as starting. The outline itself usually appears in the database about six days later, and for one fire in ten, three weeks or more later.
  • I compare burned area, not fire counts. The detection system changed in 2023 to 2024 and suddenly saw many more small fires. Counting fires across years is therefore misleading; measuring area is much safer.

For scale, the calmest of those summers burned about 110,000 hectares over the same window, roughly 7 times less.

The more interesting story is the shape of the line, because the verdict this page gave changed a lot over the summer. Here is the season month by month, each against the typical (median) amount for that same month in 2017 to 2025:

July is where 2026 broke away. By 26 July it was running at close to four times a typical season, and this page called it a lonely outlier. August then burned heavily too, but August is also when an ordinary European season does much of its own burning, so the median line climbed steeply and closed part of the gap. That is why the multiple shrank over the summer even though the fire never really let up: the yardstick moved, not the fire. September did not bring the usual slowdown, and the line kept climbing into the finish.

The biggest single day of the summer, 22 July, added about 87,000 hectares, when very large fires broke out in Ávila and in the Gironde within hours of each other. In late July that one day accounted for most of the distance between 2026 and an ordinary year. By the end of September it was only about 29% of that gap: 2026 was not one catastrophic day, it was a summer that kept producing large fires.

NoteHow final is “final”?

EFFIS maps fires as usable satellite imagery arrives, so the most recent days are the least complete and keep filling in afterwards. This is measurable. The early-September edition of this page, built from the 3 September snapshot, reported about 669,000 hectares burned between 1 June and 2 September. This final snapshot, looking at those very same dates, reports about 702,000: roughly 33,000 hectares, or 5%, showed up a month after the fact. So the last days of September on this page are still a floor, not a finished measurement, and the whole-season total will most likely creep up by a few percent before EFFIS publishes consolidated figures. That is far too small to change the summer ranking above, though it could reorder the whole-year race with 2022.

Second, the exceptions are spring flare-ups, and they are not unique to 2026: several years show bright weeks between February and April, often from fires set to clear pasture and scrub before the growing season. 2026’s spring is one of them, but 2022, 2020, 2025 and 2019 burned more between January and May. NoteReading this chart honestly

What 2026 leaves behind

In July this page asked whether 2026’s early pace would hold or ease. Four months of data later, here is what the season leaves behind.

It was a severe year, and not a lonely one. 2026 finished as the second-worst summer of the last 10, at about 63% above a typical one. The only summer ahead of it is the one just before it: two of the worst seasons in this record are now back to back, 2025 and 2026. Two years do not make a trend, but they are exactly the kind of pair worth watching in 2027.

It was made in summer. The spring that looked strange in May turned out to be an average one. What set 2026 apart was July, and above all a handful of days, with 22 July the biggest of them, when several very large fires broke out at once in different countries.

It was wide. The usual hotspots burned, but so did countries that rarely burn this much: France, Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina had their worst summer in ten years. For France, the Médoc fire at Le Porge alone was bigger than Gironde’s whole 2022 season.

It was, as always, a story of giants. Most fires were small, but the largest 1% of them held about 55% of everything that burned over the summer. A season’s total is decided less by how many fires start than by the few that escape control on the worst days.

**About the data, in one line:** these are rapid satellite outlines from
EFFIS, measured as burned *area* (never fire counts), and they will still move
by a few percent before official figures come out. Hectares are the unit
throughout; charts abbreviate thousands of hectares as **kha**.

::: {.callout-note title="How the data works (click to expand)" collapse="true"}
Satellites spot large fires, and Europe's EFFIS
service (part of the Copernicus programme) draws the outline of each burned
area it can map. I work with those outlines. I measure each fire's size
directly from its shape, using a map projection that keeps areas honest, so
hectares are comparable across countries. And I count only the parts that
fall inside Europe. The method is the same as in the [2025 season
analysis](2025.qmd) this project grew out of; that
post remains the reference for the details.

- **Most mapped fires are small, not big.** EFFIS once caught mainly fires of
  roughly 30 hectares and up, but sharper Sentinel-2 imagery now maps far
  smaller ones too, so most outlines on this page are in fact well under 30
  hectares. The very smallest fires are still missed, so totals stay below
  official statistics.
- **The outlines are rapid estimates.** They are revised as better satellite
  imagery arrives, even after the season ends. The totals below are final for
  this page, but they are not official statistics: expect EFFIS's consolidated
  figures, published later, to differ by a few percent.
- **Dates are fire dates.** Every date on this page is the day EFFIS records
  a fire as starting. The outline itself usually appears in the database about
  six days later, and for one fire in ten, three weeks or more later.
- **I compare burned area, not fire counts.** The detection system changed in
  2023 to 2024 and suddenly saw many more small fires. Counting fires across
  years is therefore misleading; measuring area is much safer.
:::

# How much of this is happening in protected nature?

One more lens on what burned: how much of it sits inside land specifically
protected for nature?

::: {.callout-note title="What is Natura 2000?"}
Natura 2000 is the European Union's network of protected natural sites,
covering habitats and species the EU has agreed are worth safeguarding. It
is not a fire ban. Many Natura 2000 landscapes, especially Mediterranean
scrub and some forest types, are fire-adapted ecosystems where occasional
burning is part of their natural cycle. A fire inside a protected site is
not automatically a disaster, but tracking how much burning happens there is
still useful context.
:::

# What 2026 leaves behind

In July this page asked whether 2026's early pace would hold or ease. Four
months of data later, here is what the season leaves behind.

**It was a severe year, and not a lonely one.** 2026 finished as
`r rank_word` summer of the last `r n_seasons`, at about
`r sprintf("%.0f%%", pct_vs_med - 100)` above a typical one.`r if (identical(as.integer(beaten_by), 2025L)) " The only summer ahead of it is the one just before it: two of the worst seasons in this record are now back to back, 2025 and 2026." else ""`
Two years do not make a trend, but they are exactly the kind of pair worth
watching in 2027.

**It was made in summer.** The spring that looked strange in May turned out
to be an average one. What set 2026 apart was July, and above all a
handful of days, with
`r paste(day(top_days$ba_date[1]), month.name[month(top_days$ba_date[1])])`

**It was wide.** The usual hotspots burned, but so did countries that rarely

`r and_list(worst_ctry)` had `r if (length(worst_ctry) > 1) "their" else "its"`
worst summer in ten years. For France, the Médoc fire at Le Porge alone was

**It was, as always, a story of giants.** Most fires were small, but the
largest 1% of them held about

A season's total is decided less by how many fires start than by the few
